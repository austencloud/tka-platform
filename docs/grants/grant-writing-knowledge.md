# Grant Writing Knowledge Base

Reference doc for the /grant skill. Not auto-loaded — read on demand during drafting.

## What Reviewers Look For

### Creative Capital Specifically
- **Boldness and originality** — risk-taking work with an original vision
- **Urgency** — "Does this need to happen NOW? Is this artist the best to execute it?"
- **Concise, jargon-free writing** — no art-speak, no dense theory
- **Early-stage projects** — they want to support development, not document completion
- **Community impact** — how will this reach people?
- **Work samples that drop you into the action** — no slow buildups, no trailers, no greatest hits reels. Continuous excerpt showing how the work unfolds.
- Projects within a year of completion are LESS likely to be funded

### General Arts Grant Panels
- Reviewers read hundreds of applications. Clear beats clever.
- Explain it like you would to a smart neighbor, not an art critic.
- Specific deliverables with timelines beat vague aspirations.
- Budget should be realistic and detailed, not padded or round-numbered.
- Work samples do more heavy lifting than prose. Choose carefully.
- Impact doesn't have to be huge — but it has to be articulable.
- Follow-through matters: how will you document, share, and measure?

## Common Rejection Reasons

1. **Art-speak and jargon** — if a non-specialist can't understand it, rewrite
2. **No clear deliverable** — "exploring the intersection of..." with no concrete output
3. **Timeline mismatch** — project needs to happen before award is announced
4. **Generic copy-paste** — same text sent to every grant, not tailored
5. **Overestimating capacity** — promising more than one person can deliver
6. **Missing the funder's priorities** — not reading what they actually fund
7. **Weak work samples** — poorly lit, wrong format, too short, not representative
8. **Hyperlinking instead of answering** — links break, reviewers won't click
9. **No urgency** — "I've been meaning to do this" vs "this must happen now because..."
10. **Tiny unreadable fonts or bad formatting** — respect the reader

## Using AI in Grant Writing (2026 Best Practices)

### DO
- Use AI to brainstorm structure and outlines
- Use AI to workshop phrasing and cut word count
- Use AI to check alignment with funder priorities
- Use AI to stress-test your pitch ("what questions would a reviewer ask?")
- Use AI to generate first drafts that YOU then rewrite in your voice
- Have AI play the role of a skeptical reviewer

### DO NOT
- Submit AI-generated text without heavy personal revision
- Let AI fabricate citations or statistics
- Use AI to generate artist statements from scratch (reviewers can tell)
- Rely on AI for budget numbers (it will hallucinate costs)
- Skip reading the actual application guidelines because AI "summarized" them

### The Voice Rule
The final text must sound like the applicant, not like a language model. Reviewers who read 500 applications develop radar for generic AI prose. Your voice — including its imperfections — is more convincing than polished AI output.

Practical approach: speak your answers out loud, transcribe, then polish. The spoken version carries your authentic voice. AI helps with structure and trimming, not with generating the core content.

## Creative Capital 2027 Application Structure

### Round I (due April 2, 2026)

| Section | Limit | Notes |
|---------|-------|-------|
| Project title | short | Descriptive, not clever |
| One-line description | 50 words / 400 chars | The elevator pitch |
| Project description | 500 words / 3,550 chars | The meat |
| Q1: Innovation | 150 words / 1,000 chars | Formal/conceptual innovation |
| Q2: Influences | 150 words / 1,000 chars | What informs the work |
| Q3: Impact | 150 words / 1,000 chars | Audiences and reach |
| Q4: Catalytic moment | 150 words / 1,000 chars | Why now, why you |
| Work sample | 1 required | Video or images of TKA in action |
| Artist bio/resume | standard | |
| Artist needs survey | ranking | Network, funding, pro dev, visibility |

### Round II (if selected)
- Itemized budget
- 1-page timeline
- Additional work samples
- Eligibility docs

### Round III
- Confirm collaborators
- Optional 100-word update

### Key Constraints
- Must be 25+ years old
- 5+ years professional practice
- Not enrolled in degree program
- Project cannot be primarily promotional or educational
- Cannot premiere before July 1, 2027

## Austen's Specific Strengths for Grant Applications

- Built a working notation system (not theoretical — it works, people use it)
- Working web application with real users
- Narrative game concept with detailed story bible
- Intersection of technology, movement arts, cultural preservation
- Solo artist with clear vision and demonstrated execution
- Chicago-based (local funding available)
- The project IS the work sample — the software demonstrates the concept

## Framing Options (adapt per funder)

**For arts funders:** "The first notation system for movement arts — a cultural preservation tool that gives flow arts the same systematic foundation that music has had for centuries."

**For tech funders:** "A real-time notation engine with a narrative game wrapper, built in Svelte/TypeScript (web app) and Unreal Engine 5 (museum game), treating movement as structured data."

**For game funders:** "A narrative walking sim set in an abandoned government archive — Museum of Jurassic Technology meets Stanley Parable — where the fake history leads to a real tool."

**For interdisciplinary funders:** "A notation system, a web application, and a video game that together argue flow arts deserves institutional seriousness — then delivers it with a wink."
