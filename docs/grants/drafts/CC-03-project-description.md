# Project Description (500 words / 3,550 chars)

You walk into an abandoned government building. The lights are on. An audio guide plays to empty rooms. Wall plaques describe the containment of "kinetic practitioners" as a public safety matter. The exhibits are meticulous. The filing system is real. The history stretches back 40,000 years.

None of it happened. But the notation system on the walls is real.

I created the Kinetic Alphabet, a system for notating prop manipulation with staves, fans, and clubs. Flow arts is spinning props in coordinated full-body patterns, often on fire. There is no standard way to write it down. Twenty-four letter forms map hand positions and movements through space. A web application I built renders animated sequences in real time. Flow artists use it today to create and share choreography. The game takes this real system and buries it inside a fabricated 40,000-year history.

The Kinetic Archive is a first-person narrative game built in Unreal Engine 5. The player walks through a museum built by a fictional agency that tracked and suppressed flow arts throughout human history. The presentation is sincere, the content is absurd, and the player cannot always tell which is which.

Two voices guide the experience. An audio narrator delivers institutional reverence, recordings looping since the building was sealed. Handwritten annotations scrawled across exhibits offer a second voice: warm, corrective, half amused. The player hears one and reads the other for the entire game without learning they belong to the same person. He recorded the narration as a young employee who believed in the mission. After four years processing suppression orders, he cracked. He leaked the classified software, sabotaged the bureaucracy, and vanished. Years later, he returned and turned the archive into an invitation. The player who notices both voices share the same dry humor arrives at the reveal on their own.

The museum is linear and chronological. Exhibits claim the notation appears in pyramid construction, medieval manuscripts, the Apollo 11 mission. Each wing's design mirrors its era: 1940s brass fixtures give way to 1970s drop ceilings, then 1990s track lighting. Mechanical wax figures perform kinetic patterns behind glass. As the timeline advances, the agency's tone shifts from clinical observation to active suppression. Filing cabinets hold transfer orders, surveillance reports, funding memos for containment programs. The bureaucrats who wrote these plaques were not entirely sure they were only dealing with spinning sticks.

In the final rooms, the institutional voice gives way to something handmade. The game ends outdoors, where the player picks up a prop for the first time. The entire museum studied movement without participation. This is the moment that breaks. A wax figure near the exit holds a museum pamphlet with a QR code linking to the real web application.

The player who finishes the game, scans the code, and opens a working notation tool cannot be entirely sure the museum was wrong.

The Kinetic Archive is the first flow arts video game. It builds a fake museum so convincing that the real thing hits harder.
