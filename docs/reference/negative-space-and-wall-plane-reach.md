# Negative Space and the Two Routes Through a Wall-Plane Reach

**Source: Austen, 2026-09-04, spoken description.** This is primary domain
material. It was not derived from code, from the MCP knowledge server, or from
any prior document, and nothing in the repository currently encodes it. He asked
that it be treated as gold and preserved as a durable reference, and said he
intends to add more descriptions like it "until we really truly understand it."

Where a phrase is load-bearing it is quoted exactly. Anything not quoted is a
structural restatement and may have drifted — correct it rather than trusting it.

---

## 1. The problem this explains

The 3D performer now grips the staff consistently. It still cannot *reach* the
positions the notation asks for.

> "when they try to go to their opposite point, in other words when their right
> hand tries to reach all the way over to the west at the same time that their
> left hand reaches over to the east, they are just unable to do that. Their
> shoulders simply are not flexible enough and they refuse."

This is not only a rig limitation. It reflects something true of bodies:

> "if they are to try to do A, B or C anyway, in real life most of the time it's
> not going to work. Their arms are going to collide."

So a solve that forces the hands to the notated points is solving the wrong
problem. Real execution takes one of the routes below.

---

## 2. Teaching B in wall plane — the crossed-arm route

Austen's "secret" for how this is actually taught:

- The arms cross into what he calls "that twisted up territory."
- The arm **coming from below** sneaks **up the armpit**.
- From there it travels a **concave path**, which gives it "a little more freedom."
- This creates "the illusion that it is occupying that corner quadrant up above
  on the non-native side."
- In reality it stays "pretty far in towards the center, because realistically it
  has to be."
- **Simultaneously**, the other hand reaches down, until both arrive at home position.

The illusion is the point: the shape reads as reaching the far quadrant while the
actual path stays much closer to the body's centre line.

## 3. The reliable alternative — body turns

> "the other option which is always reliable is using body turns, and body turns
> will present all sorts of fun stuff regarding negative space above the
> shoulder."

Austen explicitly doubts this is understood yet:

> "which I don't even know if you really truly understand"

**Open. Not modelled. Needs its own description before anything is built on it.**

---

## 4. Negative space, defined

**Setup.** Right hand at the North point, holding U, **thumb end in** — the thumb
end is the end at the north point.

**The rule.** Doing negative space means:

> "that thumb end can't pass on the downstage side of the body"

**The path instead.** The thumb end travels

> "through the pocket above my shoulder and behind my forearm, which is more
> upstage than my forearm, while trying to stay in wheel plane"

So the pocket is defined by two things at once: **above the shoulder**, and
**behind (upstage of) the forearm**. The plane constraint holds throughout.

**The grip does not stay fixed.** Halfway through:

> "the hand by the time it reaches the end is not going to be gripping it
> perpendicular anymore, it's going to be sort of sticking out in a natural way"

**Final resting place** — started thumb-in at North, ends thumb-out at East.
Viewed from above:

- the **fingers point toward the audience**
- the **wrist is rotated a little to the downstage direction**
- the staff's **pinky end is in front of the elbow**
- the **inner elbow faces up toward the sky**
- the grip is relaxed — "isn't even necessarily being gripped anymore by the
  middle, the ring and the pinky finger"
- it is "mostly just being pinched between the thumb and the index finger at that
  point"

---

## 5. The other route — no negative space

Same setup: right hand at North, thumb in. The performer decides against negative
space. Then:

- the thumb end **pushes downstage of the forearm**
- the **elbow swings back** — "not like dramatically, but it's just a natural
  consequence" — as the thumb end pushes toward East
- the **pinky end naturally starts to fall down**
- as it falls, the **elbow has to clear it by moving in the downstage direction**
- **full arm extension is required.** "by the time my arm is fully extended, which
  is what's required to get through the move this way, a full extension, my arm is
  straight"
- only then can the pinky end "clear in time to be on the other side of my body"

**It ends palm facing down** — the opposite orientation from the negative-space route.

---

## 6. The principle that fell out of it

> "I'm starting to realize that palm facing is really important element"

The two routes take the same hand from the same start to the same notated end
point, and are distinguished throughout — and terminated — by **palm facing**.
Palm facing is therefore a state variable of the reach, not a byproduct of it.

## 7. What is still open

Recorded so it is not silently assumed solved:

1. **Negative space above the shoulder during body turns** (§3). Austen named this
   as the thing least likely to be understood. Undescribed.
2. **How the concave path of §2 is parameterized** — what sets its curvature, and
   what "far in towards the center" measures against.
3. **Whether the two routes are a performer's choice or determined** by entry
   orientation, target, plane, or turn count.
4. **What forces negative space** rather than merely permitting it.
5. **Whether the relaxed thumb-and-index pinch of §4 is a distinct grip state**
   the rig must model, or an emergent consequence of the wrist rotation.
